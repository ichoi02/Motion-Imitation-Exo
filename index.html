In short

A model with 90 muscles learns to walk and run like a person. Given motors at its hips or ankles, it discovers how an exoskeleton should help it, for able-bodied gait and for gait with a weakened muscle group.

Abstract

Exoskeleton assistance learned in simulation by a muscle-driven model that imitates human gait

Designing generalizable control policies for lower-limb exoskeletons remains fundamentally constrained by exhaustive data collection or iterative optimization procedures, which limit accessibility to clinical populations. To address this challenge, we introduce a device-agnostic framework that combines physiologically plausible musculoskeletal simulation with reinforcement learning to enable scalable personalized exoskeleton assistance for both able-bodied and clinical populations.

Our control policies not only generate physiologically plausible locomotion dynamics but also capture clinically observed compensatory strategies under targeted muscular deficits, providing a unified computational model of both healthy and pathological gait. Without task-specific tuning, the resulting exoskeleton control policies produce assistive torque profiles at the hip and ankle that align with state-of-the-art profiles validated in human experiments, while consistently reducing metabolic cost across walking speeds. For simulated impaired-gait models, the learned control policies yield asymmetric, deficit-specific exoskeleton assistance that improves both energetic efficiency and bilateral kinematic symmetry without explicit prescription of the target gait pattern.

These results demonstrate that physiologically plausible musculoskeletal simulation via reinforcement learning can serve as a scalable foundation for personalized exoskeleton control across both able-bodied and clinical populations, eliminating the need for extensive physical trials.

Pipeline

One base policy, several fine-tuned policies

A single muscle-driven walking policy is trained once. Every exoskeleton and impairment condition starts from it as a fine-tune, and the hip exoskeleton policy is then distilled for hardware.

Method

A policy that has to use muscles

The policy has no joint torques of its own. It sets the excitation of 90 Hill-type muscles, and in the exoskeleton fine-tunes it also commands the exoskeleton motors.

Policy interface

Every 40 ms the policy reads the musculoskeletal state and where the reference motion is heading next, and it writes one excitation per muscle plus one torque per exoskeleton motor. Hover or tap a block to see what it holds. The layout is that of the current code; the v1 paper summarizes the same 839 numbers more briefly.

Reward

Five tracking terms keep the motion and joint moments close to the reference. Three penalties charge for muscle metabolic energy, step-to-step changes in excitation, and exoskeleton torque. Fine-tuning loosens tracking and raises the energy penalty so that assistance can change the gait.

From action to assistive torque

The exoskeleton action is scaled to the motor's torque limit (±30 N·m at the hip), rate-limited, and passed through a first-order low-pass filter that runs at the 25 Hz control rate. As a stand-in for the policy command, the demo takes the rollout's applied hip torque at 1.09 m/s and adds Gaussian action noise. Drag the cutoff to see how much of that noise reaches the joint.

Symmetry and smoothness in the actor New since arXiv v1

For the able-bodied hip exoskeleton policy, two terms are added to the actor loss and act at every gradient step. The mirror term penalizes the difference between the motor command for an observation and the mirrored command for the mirrored observation. The smoothness term penalizes changes in the motor command between consecutive states.

Simulating muscle weakness

An activation mask caps the excitation of a chosen muscle group on one side at 5% of normal. The policy is then fine-tuned to keep walking, and it finds the compensations itself. Pick a deficit.

Results

Human-like gait first, then assistance on top of it

All numbers in this section come from the arXiv v1 paper. They are simulation results for one reference subject, with metabolic cost from the Umberger (2003) and Uchida (2016) models used in v1.

Physiologically plausible imitation

The base policy tracks the joint angles and joint moments of the reference subject across walking and running. It also reproduces the vertical ground reaction force (R² 0.95), which is not a reward term.

Assistance for able-bodied gait

Fine-tuned from the same base policy, the hip and ankle exoskeleton policies produce torque profiles with the timing of profiles found by human-in-the-loop optimization, and both lower simulated metabolic cost at every speed.

Device mass: +2.9 kg hip, +3.9 kg ankle

Reduction in simulated gross metabolic cost against the no-exoskeleton baseline, averaged over the walking speeds or the running speeds of the reference clip (Section III-B of the paper). Both policies lower the cost at every speed, with larger savings at higher speeds.

Impaired gait emerges from weakness alone

With a muscle group capped at 5%, the fine-tuned policy keeps imitating healthy walking as best it can. The compensations it finds are the ones clinicians describe.

Plantarflexor weakness

Excessive dorsiflexion on the weak side and a much smaller plantarflexion moment, so push-off weakens.

Hip flexor weakness

The model hikes the hip. Pelvic tilt on the weak side peaks at 9° near toe-off to clear the swing leg, and the biological hip flexion moment drops.

Not prescribed

The reference motion is still healthy gait. Only the muscles changed, and the policy found these strategies while trying to follow it.

Deficit-specific assistance

Weakening the muscles and turning on the exoskeleton in the same fine-tune yields assistance that favors the affected side. The reference is still symmetric healthy gait and the reward has no side-specific term; the asymmetry comes from the weakened muscles alone.

Sim-to-real · new since arXiv v1

From 839 simulated signals to two gyroscopes

The hip exoskeleton policy reads muscle fiber lengths and forces that no wearable can measure. To run it on hardware, it is distilled into a student that sees only what the exoskeleton itself can sense.

The DAgger statistics and network above describe the newest student, distilled from the policy in the rollout at the top of the page; it has not run on hardware. Hardware runs 2 and 3 used an earlier, bilateral version of this TCN (one network that outputs both hip torques, 17,570 parameters), distilled from an earlier training run of the hip fine-tune (λₛ = 0.1, λₘ = 0.2). The “Simulated teacher” curves below come from that run. At walking speeds it commands about half of the current policy's peak hip torque (right hip at 1.09 m/s: 0.032 vs 0.083 N·m/kg, mean over strides).
